# 1.2.1 Types of Organizational Data

Organizations generate and store huge amounts of data every day. Some of this data is essential for running the business, while other types give the organization a competitive advantage.

If this information is stolen, altered, or destroyed, it can lead to financial loss, legal issues, or damage to the organization's reputation.

## 1.2.1.1 Traditional Data

Traditional data refers to the information that almost every organization creates and manages during its normal operations.

### Transactional Data

Transactional data records the day-to-day activities of an organization.

Examples include:

- Customer purchases
- Sales records
- Employee payroll
- Inventory updates
- Orders and invoices
- Employment records

Without transactional data, an organization would struggle to operate because it documents its everyday business activities.

### Intellectual Property (IP)

Intellectual Property (IP) is one of an organization's most valuable assets.

It includes original ideas, inventions, and creations that give a company an advantage over its competitors.

Examples include:

- Patents
- Trademarks
- Copyrighted software
- Product designs
- Research and development
- Trade secrets

For many companies, intellectual property is worth more than their physical equipment.

If competitors or cybercriminals steal this information, the company could lose years of research and millions of dollars.


### Financial Data

Financial data shows the financial health of an organization.

Examples include:

- Income statements
- Balance sheets
- Cash flow statements
- Tax records
- Budget reports

Organizations protect financial data because it contains highly sensitive information that could be abused for fraud, insider trading, or financial theft.

## Things to Remember

- Traditional data is the information organizations use to run their daily operations.
- Transactional data records business activities.
- Intellectual property gives organizations a competitive advantage.
- Financial data reflects the organization's financial condition and must be carefully protected.

# 1.2.1.2 Internet of Things (IoT) and Big Data

The **Internet of Things (IoT)** refers to a network of physical devices that are connected to the internet and can collect, share, and exchange data automatically.

These devices often contain sensors that continuously gather information from their environment.

Common examples include:

- Smartwatches
- Smart TVs
- Smart home devices
- Security cameras
- Smart cars
- Industrial sensors

Each device may only generate a small amount of data, but when millions or even billions of devices are connected, the amount of data grows extremely quickly.

This enormous collection of information is known as **Big Data**.

Big Data allows organizations to identify patterns, improve decision-making, and develop better products and services.

However, it also creates new cybersecurity challenges because more connected devices mean:

- More data to protect.
- More devices that can be attacked.
- More opportunities for cybercriminals to exploit vulnerabilities.

## Things to Remember

- IoT consists of internet-connected physical devices.
- These devices constantly collect and exchange data.
- The massive amount of information generated is called Big Data.
- More connected devices create more security risks.


# 1.2.2 The McCumber Cube

The **McCumber Cube** is a cybersecurity framework developed by **John McCumber** in **1991**.

Rather than focusing on just one aspect of security, the model encourages organizations to think about security from **three different perspectives at the same time**.

The three dimensions are:

1. Security Goals (What are we protecting?)
2. Information States (Where is the data?)
3. Security Measures (How do we protect it?)

You can think of the McCumber Cube as asking three simple questions:

- **What needs protection?**
- **Where is the information?**
- **How will we protect it?**

Together, these dimensions help organizations build a complete security strategy.


# Security Goals (CIA Triad)

The first dimension of the McCumber Cube is based on the **CIA Triad**, one of the most important concepts in cybersecurity.

> **CIA stands for Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.**

These three principles are the foundation of information security.

## Confidentiality

Confidentiality means ensuring that sensitive information is only accessible to authorized people.

In other words:

> **The right people can access the data, and everyone else cannot.**

Some common methods used to protect confidentiality include:

- Data encryption
- Strong passwords
- Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
- Identity verification
- Access control

### Example

Only a doctor should be able to view a patient's medical records.

If another patient gains access to those records, confidentiality has been violated.

## Integrity

Integrity means ensuring that data remains accurate, complete, and trustworthy.

Information should not be changed accidentally or intentionally by unauthorized users.

Common ways to protect integrity include:

- Hash functions
- Checksums
- Digital signatures
- Version control

### Example

Imagine your bank account balance is \$500.

If malware changes it to \$5,000 or \$5, integrity has been compromised because the data is no longer accurate.

## Availability

Availability means authorized users can access systems and data whenever they need them.

A system is secure only if it is both protected **and** usable.

Organizations improve availability by:

- Performing regular maintenance
- Repairing hardware
- Updating software
- Creating backups
- Building redundant systems

### Example

If an online banking website crashes during business hours, customers cannot access their accounts even though no data was stolen.

This is an availability problem.

## Quick Summary of the CIA Triad

| Principle | Main Goal |
|-----------|-----------|
| Confidentiality | Prevent unauthorized access to information. |
| Integrity | Ensure data remains accurate and unaltered. |
| Availability | Ensure systems and data are accessible when needed. |

# Information States

The second dimension of the McCumber Cube focuses on **where the data is** at any given moment.

Data can exist in three different states.

## Processing (Data in Process)

This refers to data that is actively being used.

Examples include:

- Editing a document
- Processing a payment
- Updating a database
- Running a computer program

Because the data is actively changing, it must be protected from unauthorized modification.


## Storage (Data at Rest)

Data at rest is information that has been saved on a storage device.

Examples include:

- Hard drives
- SSDs
- USB flash drives
- Cloud storage
- Databases

Even though the data is not moving, it still needs protection through encryption and access controls.


## Transmission (Data in Transit)

Data in transit is information moving between devices or networks.

Examples include:

- Sending an email
- Browsing a website
- Downloading a file
- Video conferencing

Because this data travels across networks, attackers may attempt to intercept it.

Encryption protocols such as HTTPS help protect data while it is in transit.

## Things to Remember

- Data in Process = being used.
- Data at Rest = stored.
- Data in Transit = moving across a network.

# Security Measures

The third dimension explains **how organizations protect information**.

Cybersecurity is not only about technology. It also depends on people and organizational policies.

## Awareness, Training, and Education

People are often the weakest link in cybersecurity.

Organizations reduce human error by providing security awareness training.

Employees learn how to:

- Recognize phishing emails.
- Create strong passwords.
- Protect sensitive information.
- Report suspicious activity.

Technology alone cannot stop every cyberattack if users are unaware of security risks.

## Technology

Technology includes the hardware and software used to defend information systems.

Examples include:

- Firewalls
- Antivirus software
- Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS)
- Encryption tools
- Multi-factor authentication

These tools help detect, prevent, and respond to cyber threats.

## Policies and Procedures

Policies define **what employees should do**, while procedures explain **how they should do it**.

Examples include:

- Password policies
- Incident response plans
- Backup procedures
- Acceptable use policies
- Security best practices

Clear policies help ensure that everyone in an organization follows the same security standards.

## Final Review

The McCumber Cube combines three dimensions:

| Dimension | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| CIA Triad | Defines the three goals of information security. |
| Information States | Identifies where data exists (processing, storage, transmission). |
| Security Measures | Describes how organizations protect their information. |

To fully protect information, organizations must consider **all three dimensions together**, rather than focusing on just one.

# 1.2.3 What Do You Think? — Identifying a Phishing Email

Phishing is one of the most common cybersecurity attacks because it targets the **human element** instead of directly attacking technology.


![](../../assets/Pasted%20image%2020260803141538.png)

A phishing email is a fake message designed to trick you into:

- Giving away personal information
- Clicking malicious links
- Downloading malware
- Entering login credentials on fake websites

Attackers usually pretend to be a trusted organization, such as:

- Banks
- Online services
- Companies
- Government agencies

The goal is to make the victim feel that the message is legitimate.

## Analyzing the Example Email

The email claims to come from **@Apollo Support Team**, but several warning signs reveal that it is likely a phishing attempt.

Let's examine the clues.

## 1. Customer Name

A legitimate company usually knows your name and uses proper personalization.

In the email, the greeting says:

> "Dear Ms Patel"

This alone does not prove it is phishing, because attackers can sometimes obtain names from leaked databases.

However, a generic or incorrect greeting can be a warning sign.

## 2. Link URL

This is one of the biggest red flags.

The email asks the user to click a link:

```
http://123contactform.com/contact-form-@apollo.234.54674.html
```

The link does not belong to the official @Apollo website.

A common phishing technique is creating a fake website that looks legitimate but is controlled by attackers.

Always check:

- The domain name
- Spelling mistakes
- Suspicious URLs
- Whether the website uses HTTPS

A real company would normally direct users to its official website.

## 3. Language, Spelling, and Grammar

Phishing emails often contain:

- Poor grammar
- Strange wording
- Spelling mistakes
- Unnatural sentences

For example:

> "until you validate has been changed"

This sentence does not sound like professional communication from a real company.

Attackers often create urgency quickly and may not spend time making the message perfect.

## 4. Graphics

Attackers can copy company logos and images.

A professional-looking email does not automatically mean it is real.

A fake email may contain:

- Company logos
- Similar colors
- Official-looking designs

Never trust an email only because it looks professional.

## 5. Email Address

The sender address is another important clue.

The email appears as:

```
POLLO <service@@polo.com>
```

The address does not match the company name (@Apollo).

Attackers often create fake addresses that look similar to legitimate ones.

Always check:

- The sender domain
- Extra characters
- Misspellings
- Unusual email addresses

## Phishing Email Checklist

Before clicking any link in an email, ask:

| Question | Warning Sign |
|---|---|
| Do I know the sender? | Unknown sender |
| Does the email create urgency? | "Your account will be locked" |
| Is the link suspicious? | Different domain |
| Does the grammar look professional? | Strange wording |
| Is the sender address legitimate? | Fake or unusual domain |

## Things to Remember

- Phishing attacks use deception to trick people.
- Always verify links before clicking them.
- A company logo does not prove an email is legitimate.
- Check the sender address carefully.
- Poor grammar and urgency are common phishing indicators.

# 1.2.4 Is This for Real?

Phishing and data breaches are not theoretical problems. They happen regularly, even to large and experienced organizations.

A data breach occurs when unauthorized people gain access to sensitive information.


## Real-World Example: Razer Data Breach

In 2020, gaming company **Razer** experienced a data exposure incident.

A security researcher discovered that a cloud storage system was incorrectly configured, exposing part of Razer's infrastructure to the public internet.

The exposed information affected approximately 100,000 customers.

The problem was not necessarily that attackers "hacked" the system using advanced techniques.

Instead, a security mistake caused sensitive information to become publicly accessible.

This highlights an important cybersecurity lesson:

> A system can be vulnerable even without a sophisticated attack. Simple configuration mistakes can create serious security problems.


## Why Cloud Security Matters

Modern organizations rely heavily on cloud services to store and process information.

Cloud technology provides many benefits:

- Easy access to data
- Scalability
- Lower infrastructure costs

However, organizations must properly secure cloud environments.

Common cloud security mistakes include:

- Incorrect permissions
- Exposed databases
- Weak authentication
- Misconfigured storage

A single mistake can expose thousands or millions of records.

## Things to Remember

- Data breaches can happen to large organizations.
- Security problems are not always caused by hackers; mistakes can also create vulnerabilities.
- Cloud systems require proper configuration and protection.
- Organizations must take a proactive approach to security.

# 1.2.5 Data Security Breaches

A data security breach occurs when unauthorized individuals gain access to protected information.

The consequences of a breach can affect:

- Individuals
- Businesses
- Governments
- Customers

A breach does not only mean stolen passwords.

It can involve:

- Personal information
- Financial records
- Medical data
- Intellectual property
- Customer databases

## What Should You Do After a Data Breach?

If your information may have been exposed:

1. Confirm whether your data was affected.
2. Change compromised passwords.
3. Enable multi-factor authentication.
4. Monitor financial accounts.
5. Check credit reports.
6. Report suspicious activity immediately.

Be careful during a security incident.

Attackers often create fake websites pretending to help victims recover their accounts.

A crisis situation can become another opportunity for attackers.

## Protecting Yourself

Good security habits include:

- Avoid sharing unnecessary personal information.
- Use unique passwords.
- Enable two-factor authentication.
- Be careful with emails and links.
- Monitor your accounts regularly.

## Things to Remember

- Data breaches can expose sensitive information.
- Victims should act quickly to reduce damage.
- Attackers may use security incidents as opportunities for additional scams.

# 1.2.6 Consequences of a Security Breach

A security breach can create serious consequences for an organization.

The damage is not limited to lost data.

A company may experience:

- Financial losses
- Reputation damage
- Legal problems
- Loss of customer trust
- Loss of competitive advantage

# Reputational Damage

A company's reputation can take years to build but only minutes to damage.

After a breach:

- Customers may lose trust.
- Customers may move to competitors.
- Employees may leave.
- The organization may struggle to recover.

Trust is one of the most valuable assets a company has.

# Vandalism

Cyberattacks are not always about stealing information.

Sometimes attackers modify or damage websites and systems.

Examples include:

- Changing website content
- Posting false information
- Changing contact details
- Defacing company websites

Even small changes can damage an organization's credibility.

# Theft

One of the most common consequences of a breach is data theft.

Attackers may steal:

- Personal information
- Passwords
- Credit card details
- Customer records

They may use this information for:

- Identity theft
- Financial fraud
- Selling data on illegal marketplaces

# Loss of Revenue

Security breaches can directly affect an organization's income.

Examples include:

- Website downtime preventing sales
- Lost customers
- Recovery costs
- Security improvements
- Legal penalties and fines

For some companies, a major breach can threaten their survival.

# Damaged Intellectual Property

Organizations often depend on confidential information to remain competitive.

A breach may expose:

- Trade secrets
- Product designs
- Research information
- Business strategies

If competitors obtain this information, the organization may lose its advantage.

# Things to Remember

A security breach can cause:

| Impact | Example |
|---|---|
| Reputation damage | Customers lose trust |
| Vandalism | Website content is changed |
| Theft | Personal or financial data is stolen |
| Revenue loss | Business operations are disrupted |
| Intellectual property damage | Trade secrets are exposed |

The impact of a cyberattack is often much bigger than the initial compromise. It can affect money, reputation, customers, and the future of an organization.
