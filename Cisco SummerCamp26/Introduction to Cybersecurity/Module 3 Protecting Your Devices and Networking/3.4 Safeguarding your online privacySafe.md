# 3.4.1 Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)

## What is Two-Factor Authentication?

Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) adds an extra layer of security to your account. Instead of relying only on your password, it asks for a second form of verification before allowing you to log in.

Think of it like a house with **two locks instead of one**. Even if someone steals one key (your password), they still can't get inside without the second one.

## Common Second Factors

Besides your username and password, 2FA may require:

- **Something you have**
    
    - Mobile phone
        
    - Security key (fob)
        
    - Smart card
        
- **Something you are**
    
    - Fingerprint
        
    - Face recognition
        
    - Voice recognition
        
- **A temporary code**
    
    - SMS verification code
        
    - Email verification code
        
    - Authentication app code (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, etc.)
        

## Why It Matters

Without 2FA:

- Hacker steals your password → Account compromised.
    

With 2FA:

- Hacker steals your password → They still need your second verification method.
    

This makes account theft much more difficult.

### Exam Tip

> 2FA improves security, but **it is not perfect**.

Hackers can still bypass it using:

- Phishing attacks
    
- Malware
    
- Social engineering
    

Never assume that enabling 2FA means you're completely safe.

# 3.4.2 Open Authorization (OAuth)

## What is OAuth?

OAuth (Open Authorization) lets you log in to one website using an account you already own (such as Google or Facebook) **without sharing your password** with the new website.

Example:

Instead of creating a new Cisco account, you choose:

> **Sign in with Google**

Google confirms your identity, and Cisco trusts Google's verification.

The Cisco website **never sees or stores your Google password.**

## Why OAuth is Useful

- Fewer passwords to remember.
    
- Faster sign-in.
    
- Your password stays with the original provider (Google, Microsoft, Apple, etc.).
    

## Simple Example

Without OAuth:

You create:

- New username
    
- New password
    

More passwords = More chances to forget or reuse weak passwords.

With OAuth:

- Click "Continue with Google."
    
- Google verifies you.
    
- You're logged in.
    

Easy and more secure.

# 3.4.3 Sharing Information Online (Scenario)

You updated your job position online, and the social media site asks for more profile information.

## Question

**Which information is generally safe to share?**

**Correct Answers:**

- Profile picture
    
- Organization/company name
    

## Information You Should Avoid Sharing

Never share unnecessary personal information such as:

- Mobile phone number
    
- Email address
    
- Date of birth
    
- Your manager's information
    
- Colleagues' contact information
    

The more personal details you publish, the easier it becomes for attackers to:

- Build your profile
    
- Guess passwords
    
- Answer security questions
    
- Launch phishing attacks
    
- Commit identity theft
    

### Rule to Remember

> Share only what is necessary. Every extra piece of information is another clue for attackers.

 

# 3.4.4 Don't Get Spoofed

## What is Email Spoofing?

Email spoofing is when an attacker **fakes the sender's email address** to make a message appear as if it came from someone you trust.

For example:

Instead of:

```
support@paypal.com
```

An attacker may send:

```
support-paypal@gmail.com
```

or make the email appear to come from PayPal even though it doesn't.

The goal is usually to trick you into:

- Clicking malicious links
    
- Downloading malware
    
- Entering passwords
    
- Sending money
    

## Another Privacy Risk

Many people don't realize that:

- Anyone with access to your device can view your browsing history.
    
- Emails are **not automatically encrypted** while traveling across the Internet.
    

That's why protecting your browser and email privacy is important.


# 3.4.5 Email and Web Browser Privacy

## Private Browsing

Most web browsers include a private browsing mode.

Different browsers use different names:

|Browser|Private Mode|
|  |---|
|Google Chrome|Incognito|
|Microsoft Edge / Internet Explorer|InPrivate|
|Mozilla Firefox|Private Window|
|Safari|Private Browsing|

## What Private Browsing Does

Private mode helps by:

- Not saving browsing history
    
- Not storing cookies after you close the session
    
- Not saving search history
    
- Not remembering form entries
    

Useful when using:

- Shared computers
    
- Public computers
    
- School or work devices
    

## What Private Browsing Does NOT Do

Many people misunderstand Incognito mode.

It **does not**:

- Hide you from your ISP
    
- Hide you from your employer
    
- Hide you from websites
    
- Hide you from hackers on an insecure network
    
- Make you anonymous
    

It only prevents **your own device** from keeping local browsing records.

### For Better Privacy

Use:

- HTTPS websites
    
- VPN on public Wi-Fi
    
- Strong passwords
    
- Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)
    
- Keep your browser updated
    

### Exam Tip

Remember the difference:

- **Private Browsing** = Hides browsing activity from **other users of your device.**
    
- **VPN** = Encrypts your Internet traffic while it's traveling across the network.
    
- **2FA** = Protects your account if your password is stolen.
