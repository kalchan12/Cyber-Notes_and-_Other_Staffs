## 1.0.3 Definition of AI

**Alan Turing and the Turing Test:**

- Turing is one of the founders of theoretical computer science; he provided the mathematical theory behind the modern computer
- In 1950, Turing wrote a paper asking "Can machines think?"
- He proposed the **imitation game**: a human observer holds text-only conversations with both a human and a machine
- The observer cannot see or hear them, so the decision is based purely on the quality of the text conversation
- If the observer cannot tell the machine apart from the human, the machine demonstrates the ability to think
- This imitation game is now called the **Turing Test**
- Historical note: the movie *The Imitation Game* tells the story of Turing's WWII team decrypting Nazi Germany's military communications

**John McCarthy and the term "AI":**

- The exact term **artificial intelligence** was coined by John McCarthy, a founder of computer science
- McCarthy and other scientists met at Dartmouth College to propose studying whether machines can be designed to simulate intelligence
- They based their research on the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can, in principle, be described precisely enough that a machine can simulate it
- McCarthy formally defined AI as **"the science and engineering of making intelligent machines"**
- He defined intelligence as "the computational part of the ability to achieve goals in the world" - in other words, the ability to perform useful tasks

**Breaking down the definition:**

- AI is a field of research that began in the 1950s with the goal of getting machines to perform tasks that require intelligence
- **Artificial / machine** - not performed by biological intelligence (a human, dog, or panda)
- **Task-oriented** - the machine takes some action (output a number or text) to achieve a useful outcome
- **Intelligence** - the ability to make decisions even in ambiguous circumstances, and to learn from experience
- Intelligence is often defined in terms of what humans can do, though it does not have to be limited to human intelligence

## 1.0.4 Intelligence

Some tasks require more intelligence than others. The video walks through a spectrum of photo-editing tasks:

- **Grayscale conversion** - apply a math formula averaging red, green, and blue. Accomplished consistently with math, so it requires little intelligence
- **Cropping to a given size** - follow simple rules for the exact size and shape, so it requires little intelligence
- **Choosing how to crop** - more nuanced: the computer must figure out what to keep (e.g., the person) and what to leave out, so it requires more intelligence
- **Identifying objects** - harder with a few simple instructions because many objects are round and white; you would need countless rules and exceptions, so it requires more intelligence
- **Removing an object** (e.g., an eraser tool that fills in what would reasonably be behind it) - would require a huge number of instructions and exceptions, so it requires the most intelligence

**Key ideas:**

- Intelligence can be thought of as the ability to perform tasks humans do well, such as understanding images, text, and language
- Think of tasks on a **spectrum of difficulty**: easier tasks need fewer rules, harder tasks need more rules and exceptions
- A person or computer that performs well on difficult tasks can **handle ambiguity** - situations it has not encountered before - and still achieve a reasonable outcome
- In the early days of AI research, writing these rules let machines perform useful tasks with images, text, and numbers
- But when giving lots of rules is not enough, a different approach is needed: **machine learning**

## 1.0.5 Machine Learning

**The baking analogy:**

- One way to learn to bake a cake: get a cookbook and follow the instructions step by step (rules-based)
- Another way: watch a friend or someone on YouTube and mimic what they do (learning from examples)
- Machine learning is the AI approach that learns from data and examples instead of rules

**History:**

- AI researchers from the 1950s-1980s mainly worked on **symbolic AI** - coming up with ever more complex instructions for machines (the rules-based approach)
- At the same time, another approach called **machine learning** was based on statistics, trying to get computers to learn from data and examples
- The term machine learning was coined by **Arthur Samuel**, an IBM researcher who trained a computer to play checkers - not by giving it instructions, but by having it play against itself and learn which moves gave more points over time

**Tom Mitchell's formal definition:**

- Simplified version: a computer program learns from experience with respect to some task if its performance on that task improves with experience
- Full version: a program is said to learn from experience **E** with respect to some class of tasks **T** and performance measure **P**, if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E
- In other words: a machine learns from experience if it performs a task, gets objective feedback on how well it did, and uses that feedback to get better

**The learning loop (panda example):**

1. Give the machine an image and ask it to output "panda" or "not panda"
2. It knows nothing about pandas, but performs the task as best it can (outputs "not panda")
3. You give it the correct answer ("panda"); it notes the mistake and makes adjustments
4. Repeat with many images - probably 100 to 1000 - always giving the correct answer only after the machine has tried
5. Over time, it gets better at predicting correctly, even on new images it has never seen

**Try it yourself:** Google's **Teachable Machine** (free website). Steps: click Get Started, choose Image Project, pick the standard image model, create two classes (e.g., panda and dog), record webcam photos of each at different angles, click Train Model, then test with new objects. It outputs probabilities for each class.

## 1.0.6 Models

**The toaster oven analogy:**

- A mini toaster oven has knobs to control how hot and how long to toast
- Machine learning models have **parameters** you can adjust, similar to those knobs, so you get the toast you want

**What is a model?**

- A model is a **function**: it takes input, processes it, and produces output
- A model is special because it can *learn to change* how it processes input to produce output
- Think of a "smart toaster oven" that customizes temperature and timing based on the bread it receives

**Vocabulary:**

- **Features** - the inputs the model uses (e.g., bread length, width, and type)
- **Parameters** - numbers (like 0.5 or 0.75) that start out randomly assigned and get adjusted as the model learns; represented as gears you can turn
- **Label** - the correct answer for a particular example of inputs (e.g., the ideal piece of toast for a given sourdough)
- **Performance measure** - outputs a number quantifying how good the model's output is (comparing output to the label)
- **Optimizer** - a tool (like a wrench) that uses the performance measure to update the model's parameters so the next output is better
- More **data** (more kinds of bread) helps the model learn to generalize to new cases

**AI is many categories of models** (not one single entity, despite how the news sounds):

- Models for **structured data** - numbers and categories (e.g., kinds of bread)
- **Computer vision** - images and video
- **Automatic speech recognition** - audio in, text out (voice dictation on your phone/computer)
- **Audio generation** - e.g., text to speech
- **Natural language processing (NLP)** - handling human language in text, plus recognizing and generating speech

**Neural networks:**

- A major category of models, likely behind many apps today, because they perform well with lots of data
- Fundamental unit: a **neuron**, which contains **weights** - another name for parameters, specific to neural networks
- Neurons combine into **layers**; each layer takes the previous layer's output as input
- Many layers = **deep** network, hence the term **deep learning** - machine learning using neural networks
- Fun fact: if you know how to draw a neural network, add a blue line and orange circle and you can draw the Golden Gate Bridge

**Training vs. inference:**

- **Training phase**: the model, performance measure, and optimizer work together while the model learns from data that includes correct answers (labels)
- **Inference phase**: once trained, the model is used by application users; there is no label data, and the performance measure and optimizer are no longer used
- During inference, parameters are **not changed** by your inputs - the app is not learning from you every time you use it
- Companies may collect your inputs and aggregate them over time, clean the data, identify correct answers, and later train a model separately
- Main point: when you use a machine learning model, it is almost never updating its parameters every minute or second

**Big picture recap:**

- **AI** is a field of research that enables computers to perform tasks intelligently
- **Machine learning** is a major subfield of AI that uses data to train computers
- A **model** is a function that performs a task by taking an input and producing an output, and can learn from experience and data to get better
- Don't worry if you didn't catch everything - the rest of the course focuses on hands-on practice with AI apps
