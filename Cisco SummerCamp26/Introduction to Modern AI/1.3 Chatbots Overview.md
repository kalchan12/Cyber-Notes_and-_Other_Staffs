# 1.3 Chatbots Overview

## 1.3.1 What you can do with Chatbots

**Vocabulary:**

- **Prompt** - the input you give the chatbot
- **Completion** (or response) - the output the chatbot produces

**The 7 categories of chatbot tasks:**

1. **Expand** - answer questions and generate ideas (e.g., "Who is BTS?")
2. **Synthesize** - summarize and label text (topics, sentiment)
3. **Transform** - translate and reword text
4. **Evaluate** - fact check and score content
5. **Chat** - have ongoing conversations with follow-ups; the chatbot can even initiate
6. **Take actions** - personal assistants (calendar events, text messages) and internet search with source links
7. **Multimodal input** - combine images with text (e.g., a photo of a panda-like bun → "Is this a panda?")

## 1.3.2 Predict the Next Word

**The logic problem experiment:**

- Problem: "10 people enter a cafe with 9 seats. Can everyone sit?" → the chatbot answers "No" correctly
- Adding "Give your answer, then reasoning" → the chatbot answered "Yes" (wrong!)
- Adding "Give reasoning, then answer" → it answered "No" (correct)
- The order of your instructions changed the quality of the result

**Why order matters:**

- Chatbots predict the **next word** one at a time
- After each word, the output is appended to the input, and the process repeats
- Because each new word is conditioned on everything before it, the structure of your prompt changes the whole response
- Example: "twinkle twinkle little" → "star, how I wonder what you are..."

**Language modeling:**

- **Language modeling** = the task of predicting the next word
- A **Language Model** = a model that has learned to predict the next word
- **LLM** = Large Language Model - large because of both the model itself and the huge amount of training data
- LLMs are general: the same model can translate, summarize, answer questions, and more
- A **prompt** is an instruction embedded in the input text (e.g., "Translate to French: ...")

**Practical tip:**

- Ask the chatbot to do helpful subtasks first (e.g., "reason through it, then give the answer") and it often does better
- Similarly, "write an outline, then the article" tends to produce better articles

## 1.3.3 Hallucinations

**Definition:** a chatbot making up something that is incorrect - including going off-topic instead of answering your question.

**The Paris Olympics example:**

- A chatbot asked about 2024 Paris Olympics medal counts was trained before the event happened (its **training cutoff** predates the data)
- It hallucinated: gold → Tebogo, silver → Bednarek, bronze → Lyles (all incorrect - the real results were different)
- The model wasn't lying maliciously - it was generating plausible-sounding text based on patterns

**Randomness:**

- OpenAI's Playground shows the probabilities of the next word
- When generating, the model samples randomly - it doesn't always pick the most likely word
- Example: "A city I want to visit is" produced San Diego, Tokyo, Rome, Seattle, Amsterdam, and Paris across runs - all different and all valid
- Randomness is why you get different answers each time

**Spotting hallucinations:**

- Any factual claim that is wrong = hallucination
- Easier to spot in domains you know well - e.g., "Who is Eddy Shyu?" → the chatbot confidently described a YouTuber who doesn't exist
- Going off-topic also counts: ask about Paris Olympics 2024 and it may answer about the 1900 or 1924 Olympics instead

**Practice:** test prompts on topics you know, fact check the responses, and observe when it drifts off-topic.

## 1.3.4 Memory

**What memory is:**

- **Memory** = software built around the LLM that saves the history of prompts and completions (the conversation)
- Example: ask "Who is Steve Jobs?", then "Who is Eddy Shyu?" - the second answer followed the previous topic (called him an entrepreneur)
- Start a new chat: "Who is Taylor Swift?", then "Who is Eddy Shyu?" - the answer followed music (called him a violinist)
- Either way, the whole conversation becomes part of the input to the model

**Important points:**

- The model itself doesn't remember anything - memory is separate software that re-sends the conversation history
- Memory is per-user: your chat history is your own
- The model's parameters are **not** updated based on your conversation
- Why not just update the model per user? Because the model is shared - everyone's updates would affect everyone else
- **Tip:** start a new conversation when switching topics (e.g., asking about pineapple cake in a chat about dogs)

## 1.3.5 Level of Difficulty

**Task difficulty varies:**

- **Narrow vs. multi-step**: "give 10 ways to say I love you" is narrow; "write a movie script" is multi-step and much harder
- **Open-ended vs. single correct answer**: a cake recipe has many valid answers; a math problem has one
- **Data availability**: English↔Spanish translation is easy (huge amounts of training data); Latvian↔Nepali is hard (little data)

**Practical tips:**

- For rare language pairs, translate via English as an intermediate step (e.g., Latvian → English → Nepali) - it usually works better
- Check the output more carefully for hard tasks
- Use a more powerful model for harder tasks (more capable models handle them better)

## 1.3.6 Use Multiple Chatbots

**Proprietary (hosted) chatbots:**

- Examples: ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot, Mistral
- Cannot be downloaded - they run on company servers, free with limits; pay for more usage

**Open source / open commercial license:**

- Examples: Meta AI (Llama), Command R+ (Cohere)
- Can be downloaded and modified
- Note: Llama's open commercial license requires you to get a license from Meta if your app exceeds 700 million monthly users

**HuggingChat:**

- Hosts open models online: Llama, Command R+, Mixtral (the "x" denotes a mixture of experts), Phi
- Try the same prompt on different models and compare answers

**Running models on your own computer:**

- Your computer's RAM must hold the model - typical computers have 8-16 GB
- Models come compressed: Llama 3.1 8B is ~16 GB uncompressed, ~4 GB compressed
- **LM Studio** lets you download and run compressed models locally (Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma, Phi, Mistral 7B, Mixtral)
- **Leaderboards** (e.g., Hugging Face) rank models by quality - human volunteers vote; rankings change weekly

**Why use multiple chatbots:**

- Rankings change over time - today's best isn't always best
- Free tier limits push you to alternate between providers
- Different models have different behaviors, features, and personalities

**Why use smaller models:**

- **Carbon footprint**: data centers consume enormous electricity; smaller models are greener
- **Privacy**: with hosted chatbots, your prompts go to the company's servers; local models keep everything on your machine
- **Offline use**: works without internet
- Less energy overall

## 1.3.7 Test it Before you Rely on it

**The Shih-Tzu article experiment:**

- The instructor gave the chatbot a web link about Shih-Tzu dogs and asked it to extract text
- The chatbot quoted things like "double layered" and "almond" eyes - but those words did not appear in the article
- It paraphrased and filled in from its own knowledge instead of quoting the article
- On HuggingChat with Llama and the Fetch URL tool: "owl head" and "double coat" *were* verified present in the article

**Practical lessons:**

- Chatbots can say they did something when they didn't ("it says it did" ≠ it did)
- Test a simpler version of your task first
- Verify outputs when accuracy matters (e.g., direct quotes, facts)

## 1.3.8 How to Write your Prompts

**Language:**

- Prompt in your own language - chatbots work in many languages (Chinese, French, etc.)
- Mistral AI is French-based and trained to perform well in European languages (French, German, Italian, Spanish)

**Be detailed:**

- Write more text than you would for a web search: give detail, ask for intermediate steps, say what you'll do with the information
- Break the task into subtasks in a logical order (e.g., for comparing two dog breeds: review the reference → list similarities and differences → suggest how to tell them apart)
- Research first, conclusions after

**Specify the output format:**

- Ask for a table with specific rows/columns, or bullet points
- Gemini can even export a table to Google Sheets

**Set expectations:**

- Tell the chatbot it can say "I don't know" - a good answer may be admitting uncertainty
- Ask for a single answer when you want one

**Add structure to your prompt:**

- Subheaders (e.g., "References", "Steps"), numbering, and bullet points help the model organize its response
- **Delimiters** separate your content from your instructions:
  - Angle brackets for links: <...>
  - Three double quotes or three hashtags around the text to process
- Cohere has a web scraper tool: paste a URL, it fetches and processes the page; scraping twice can produce better extraction - highlight the output to see references

**Personas:**

- Give the chatbot a role: a **veterinarian** persona answers in a structured, professional way; a **comedian** persona answers casually and jokes around
- Mistral NeMo (Mistral AI + NVIDIA) demonstrates persona differences well

**Few-shot prompting:**

- **Few-shot** = give examples of the input and expected output in your prompt
  - Example: guitar/fish → "you can't tune a fish"; dressed dog/unicycle → "you can't ride a dog" (wait, the prompt says dressed dog/unicycle → "what to wear to a unicycle race")
  - The chatbot learns the pattern from the examples
- **Zero-shot** = no examples, just the instruction
- Add an instruction like "Study how these questions are answered" then "Now answer the following question" to improve consistency

## 1.3.9 Silver Linings

**Meaning:** something good comes from something bad - a "silver lining."

**The chatbot as a silver linings coach:**

- First, **emotional validation** - acknowledge the person's feelings
- **Toxic positivity** = jumping to "look on the bright side" without acknowledging the negative
- A good coach acknowledges the cloud too, then looks for silver linings

**Silver lining categories:**

- **Time** - losing something frees up time for other things
- **Environment change** - losing a situation opens opportunities elsewhere
- **Opportunity cost** - economics term: the cost of *not* doing other things; when one option ends, other options become affordable

**Steve Jobs example:**

- Getting fired from Apple was "the best thing that could have happened" - it freed him to start NeXT and Pixar, and eventually led to his return to Apple
- The example shows how a silver lining framework can frame even difficult events positively
