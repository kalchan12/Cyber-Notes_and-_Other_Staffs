# 1.1 Computer Vision

## 1.1.1 Object Detection

**Starting point:** when you view a photo in Google Photos, tapping the lens icon draws a rectangle around an object it detected.

**Key concepts:**

- **Bounding box** - a rectangle drawn around the object of interest. Rectangles are efficient because any rectangle can be defined with just two points (top-left and bottom-right)
- **Object localization** - defining bounding boxes (finding *where* an object is)
- **Image classification** - naming or labeling the image (*what* is in it). The possible outputs are called **classes** (e.g., person, flower, dog, bird)
- **Object detection** = localization + classification: for each bounding box, the model says both where the object is and what class it belongs to

**Class specificity:**

- The labels you get depend on how specific the classes are
- Example: flower → Dahlia → Dahlia pinnata → Golden Torch Dahlia (a variety)
- Photos can be labeled at different levels of specificity

**Camera angle matters:**

- The same dog (Tofu) photographed from the front and from the side can be labeled as different breeds
- Different angles reveal different features, so the model's classification can change
- Adjusting the bounding box manually can improve results - e.g., drawing a tight box around the penguin (not including the snow) improved the classification

## 1.1.2 Similarity Search

Below the classification results in Google Photos, you'll see **similar images**.

**How it works:**

- Represent an image with numbers: e.g., two lines, one for "amount of brown fur" and one for "amount of white fur"
- A dog with more brown fur sits at one position on the number lines, one with more white fur sits elsewhere
- Dogs with similar fur appear close together in this space - that's how similar images are found
- In practice, machine learning models choose the features automatically; we just need the images and some labels

**Embeddings:**

- An **embedding** is a compact list of numbers (a few hundred to a few thousand) representing an image
- By comparison, a photo might have 9 million pixels times 3 (red, green, blue), so the embedding is far more compact
- **Distance** between two embeddings = how similar the images are: small distance = similar, large distance = different
- This applies not just to images but also to audio and text

**Image classification vs. similarity search:**

- Classification model: outputs the class label (e.g., "bird")
- Similarity search model: creates an embedding, compares it against embeddings in a database, and returns the closest matches
- Two-stage process: model creates embedding → another program compares against stored embeddings → returns similar images

## 1.1.3 Image Segmentation

**The portrait effect** (bokeh): the subject stays sharp while the background is blurred - this requires knowing *which pixels* belong to the subject.

**Key concepts:**

- **Pixels** - the tiny dots that make up a digital image; each pixel has a red, green, and blue value, so a photo is essentially a spreadsheet of RGB numbers
- **Image segmentation** - the task of identifying, per pixel, whether it is part of the foreground (subject) or background
- Depth perception is related but not required for the portrait effect

**How it's done:**

1. The model outputs a **mask**: an image the same dimensions as the original, where each pixel is 0 or 1 (1 = part of the subject)
2. Multiply the original photo pixels by the mask - background pixels become 0 (black), subject stays
3. Blur the *entire* photo
4. Overlay the sharp subject onto the blurred photo → the bokeh effect

**Why not just blur the background directly?**

- Blurring near the border of the subject would bleed into the subject and make its outline blurry
- The mask approach keeps the subject perfectly sharp with clean edges

## 1.1.4 OCR

**Starting point:** in Google Photos, lens view → select text → copy it out of the photo.

**Key concepts:**

- **OCR (Optical Character Recognition)** - detects characters in an image and converts them to editable text
- Process: bounding boxes around characters, then classification of each character (letters, punctuation, digits)
- OCR is essentially an application of object detection applied to characters

**Limitations:**

- Curved text can confuse the model - e.g., the Hollywood sign text read top-to-bottom when wrapped around a circle
- This shows the model may fail where a human wouldn't; always spot-check the output
- Failures are learning opportunities - understanding why a model fails helps you use it better

**Use cases:**

- Scanning a receipt: extract item names and prices
- Translating a sign: OCR + translation overlaid on the image
- In Chrome, search with Google Lens lets you copy, translate, or search selected text

## 1.1.5 Take a Break (Incubation Theory)

**Two approaches to a hard problem:**

- Keep grinding (work on it continuously)
- Take a break - psychologists call this **incubation**

**What psychologists think happens during breaks (incubation theory):**

- **Unconscious thought** - your mind keeps processing the problem in the background while you daydream or think of other things
- **Remote association** - you combine familiar concepts in new ways (e.g., mixing ice cream with cake to invent a new dessert)
- **Rest and recovery** - your brain restores energy for the next attempt
- **Selective forgetting** - you let go of dead-end ideas you were stuck on, freeing up space for new approaches

**The four stages of problem solving:**

1. **Preparation** - study the problem, try approaches
2. **Incubation** - take a break: do other tasks, exercise, or even sleep/nap
3. **Illumination** - the idea arrives ("aha!" moment)
4. **Verification** - work out the idea and confirm it works

**Takeaway:** if you're stuck on a problem, take a break - it's not wasted time, it's part of the process.
