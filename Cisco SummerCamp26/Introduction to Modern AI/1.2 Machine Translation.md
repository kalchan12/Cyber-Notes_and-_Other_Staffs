# 1.2 Machine Translation

## 1.2.1 Words and Word Order

**Why "llamas" is tricky:**

- "llamas" can mean flames, llamas (the animals), or "llamas" as in "como te llamas" (what's your name?)
- Meaning depends on context, which makes machine translation harder than a simple word swap

**Word-level translation (bilingual dictionary):**

- Simple words map cleanly: salad = ensalada, eat = comer, fresh = fresco
- **Homonyms** - words with the same spelling and pronunciation but different meanings (e.g., wave, match, spring, lie, bark, bank, right, light, tire, park, can)
  - light = la luz (light in the room) or ligera (a light meal)
  - bank = banco (bank) or orilla (river bank); banco also means bench
- **Homographs** - same spelling, different meanings, may or may not share pronunciation (e.g., wind, tear, bow, contract, content, lead)
- Dictionaries alone can't handle these; context is required

**Word order differences:**

- English is **SVO** (subject-verb-object): "I ate the salad"
- Hindi is **SOV**: "I the salad ate" (subject-object-verb)
- Japanese also puts the verb after the object
- **Adjective placement**: English puts adjectives before the noun (as do Chinese, Japanese, Korean); Spanish puts them after (as do French, Arabic, Hindi)
- **Pro-drop languages**: Spanish, Chinese, and Japanese can drop the subject ("yo" = I) when it's clear from context
- You don't need to memorize the linguistic terms - just be aware that such differences exist

## 1.2.2 Context

**The French tu/vous example:**

- French has two words for "you": **tu** (informal) and **vous** (formal or plural)
- There are also associated forms: t' (short for te), ton (your, informal), votre (your, formal)
- Translating a sentence with "you" is ambiguous: should it be informal or formal?

**Testing with a model:**

- Translating a sentence about a queen into French, the model used the informal forms - it couldn't tell the context from the sentence alone
- **Context** = the surrounding words that help determine the meaning of a sentence
- Providing context hints changed the model's behavior:
  - Changing "you" to "vous" in the instruction (adding a [formal] hint) → formal forms used
  - Capitalizing "Queen" (proper noun) also triggered the formal register
  - Applying all these fixes at once made the model translate everything formally
- Key insight: the model is not following explicit rules a programmer wrote; it learned patterns from data - experiment to understand its behavior

## 1.2.3 Idiomatic vs. Literal

**Idioms** - expressions with both a literal meaning and a figurative meaning:

- Examples: piece of cake, spill the beans, kick the bucket, etc.

**The "piece of cake" example:**

- Translating "a piece of cake" to Italian: literal translation "un pezzo di torta" (a slice of cake) vs. the idiomatic "un gioco da ragazzi" (a child's game)
- Context changes the translation:
  - "It was a piece of cake" → idiomatic (it was easy)
  - "She asked me if I could eat the grapefruit" + "a piece of cake" → literal (an actual cake slice)
  - "It was a piece of cake" with a period → idiomatic again
- The literal meaning is best translated with the literal equivalent; the idiomatic meaning needs the target language's idiom

**Idiomatic equivalents in other languages:**

- Italian: "gioco da ragazzi" (child's play)
- French: "c'est du gâteau" (it's cake)
- Arabic: "easy as drinking water"
- Chinese: "小菜一碟" (a side dish)
- Japanese: "朝飯前" (before breakfast)
- Spanish: "pan comido" (eaten bread)
- These are all different words but the same idea: it was easy

**Takeaway:** models aren't following strict rules - they combine probabilities. Try out your own favorite idiom and see what the model does with it.

## 1.2.4 Distance between Two Words

**Spanish gender agreement:**

- Spanish articles agree with the noun's gender: lo (masculine) / la (feminine)
- Example: "salad" = la ensalada (feminine); the pronoun "it" referring to the salad should be "la"

**What happens when words are far apart:**

- In a short sentence, the model correctly uses "la" for "it" (referring to la ensalada) and "deliciosa" (feminine adjective)
- Inserting extra text between "salad" and "it" breaks the link - the model then defaults to masculine "lo" and "delicioso"
- **Distance matters**: the farther apart two related words are, the harder it is for the model to connect them

**Coreference resolution:**

- The NLP term for figuring out when two pieces of text refer to the same thing (e.g., "salad" and "it")
- It's harder when the referring words are far apart in the text

## 1.2.5 Think Like a Detective

**How to explore machine translation:**

- Interact with the model and observe its behavior - it's like playing a game to learn its rules
- Test translations piece by piece, changing one thing at a time to isolate what matters

**The cake investigation (putting it together):**

- "I realized it was a piece of cake" → idiomatic translation (realized = understood)
- "I recognized it was a piece of cake" → literal translation (recognized = saw/knew it was an actual cake)
- "I could see it was a piece of cake" → idiomatic again (breaks the pattern!)
- The model's behavior isn't perfectly predictable - that's why you test

**How to test effectively:**

- Use inputs where you know what the expected output should be (like the French tu/vous examples)
- Change one variable at a time and observe the difference
- This approach applies to chatbots too, not just translation
