
# 1.4.1 Types of Attackers

Not every hacker has the same goal. Some attack systems to help improve security, while others do it for money, politics, or simply for fun.

Cyber attackers are commonly grouped based on **their skill level, motivation, and intentions.**

## Script Kiddies (Amateurs)

**Script kiddies** are inexperienced hackers who rely on tools or attack scripts created by others instead of developing their own.

They usually have limited technical knowledge but can still cause serious damage.

### Their Characteristics

- Use ready-made hacking tools.
- Download exploits from the internet.
- Often attack out of curiosity, boredom, or to show off.
- Usually don't fully understand how the tools work.

### Example

A teenager downloads a DDoS tool from GitHub and uses it to take down a gaming server.

> Think of them as someone who can drive a race car but has no idea how the engine works.

## Hackers (White, Gray, and Black Hats)

Hackers are usually classified by **their intentions**, not their technical skills.

### White Hat Hackers (Ethical Hackers)

White hat hackers use their skills to **help organizations improve security.**

They always have permission before testing a system.

Their goal is to find vulnerabilities before criminals do.

### What They Do

- Perform penetration testing.
- Conduct security assessments.
- Report vulnerabilities.
- Help fix security weaknesses.

### Example

A company hires an ethical hacker to test its website for security flaws.

**Remember: Permission + Good Intentions = White Hat**

### Gray Hat Hackers

Gray hat hackers fall somewhere between white hats and black hats.

They usually access systems **without permission**, but they don't always have malicious intentions.

Sometimes they:

- Report vulnerabilities to the owner.
- Publicly disclose security flaws.
- Request payment before revealing how to fix the issue.

Because they act without authorization, their actions can still be illegal.

### Example

Someone finds a vulnerability in a website without permission and emails the company asking for a reward.

**Remember: Good intentions don't automatically make an action legal.**
### Black Hat Hackers

Black hat hackers are the criminals of the cybersecurity world.

Their goal is personal gain or causing harm.

They attack systems to:

- Steal money.
- Steal data.
- Install malware.
- Commit fraud.
- Sell stolen information.
- Damage organizations.

### Example

A hacker steals millions of customer credit card numbers and sells them on the dark web.

**No permission + Malicious intentions = Black Hat**

## Organized Hackers

Some cyberattacks are far too large for a single person.

Organized hacking groups often have funding, specialized skills, and clear objectives.

They include several different types of attackers.

### Cybercriminal Organizations

These groups attack organizations for financial gain.

Examples include:

- Ransomware gangs
- Identity theft groups
- Credit card fraud rings

### Hacktivists

Hacktivists use hacking as a form of political or social protest.

Their goal is usually to spread a message rather than make money.

They may:

- Deface websites.
- Leak information.
- Disrupt online services.

### State-Sponsored Hackers

These attackers work on behalf of governments.

Their missions often involve:

- Cyber espionage
- Intelligence gathering
- Sabotage
- Attacking critical infrastructure

They are usually among the most skilled and well-funded attackers.

## Things to Remember

| Attacker | Main Goal |
|  --------|-----------|
| Script Kiddie | Curiosity, fun, showing off |
| White Hat | Improve security with permission |
| Gray Hat | Find vulnerabilities without permission, usually not for personal gain |
| Black Hat | Money, theft, damage, illegal activities |
| Organized Hackers | Financial, political, or national objectives |

# 1.4.2 What Color Is My Hat?

This activity tests whether you can identify an attacker's motivation based on a scenario.

Instead of memorizing definitions, ask yourself one question:

> **Did they have permission, and what was their goal?**

 

## Scenario 1

**A hacker broke into ATM systems, found vulnerabilities, and worked with the manufacturer to fix them.**

### Correct Answer

**White Hat**

### Why?

Although they hacked into the system, their goal was to improve security and help the manufacturer fix the weaknesses.


## Scenario 2

**A hacker stole \$10 million using stolen banking credentials.**

### Correct Answer

**Black Hat**

### Why?

The attacker committed financial theft for personal gain.

## Scenario 3

**Someone's job is to identify weaknesses in a company's computer systems.**

### Correct Answer

**White Hat**

### Why?

Ethical hackers are hired specifically to improve an organization's security.

## Scenario 4

**A hacker used malware to steal customer credit card information and sold it.**

### Correct Answer

**Black Hat**

### Why?

The attack involved theft, malware, and financial profit.

## Scenario 5

**A researcher accidentally discovered a vulnerability in a system they were authorized to access.**

### Correct Answer

**White Hat**

### Why?

The researcher already had permission to access the system and discovered the vulnerability while performing authorized work.


## Quick Trick for Exams

- **Permission + Improving security = White Hat**
- **No permission + Criminal activity = Black Hat**
- **No permission + Mixed intentions = Gray Hat**

# 1.4.3 Internal and External Threats

Cybersecurity threats don't always come from anonymous hackers on the internet.

Sometimes the biggest threat comes from **inside the organization.**

## Internal Threats

An **internal threat** comes from someone who already has authorized access to an organization's systems.

This could include:

- Employees
- Former employees
- Contractors
- Interns
- Business partners

Because these people already have access, they may not need to "hack" into the system.

### Internal threats can be:

**Intentional**

Someone deliberately steals data or sabotages systems.

**Accidental**

Someone clicks a phishing email, misconfigures a server, or accidentally leaks sensitive information.

### Examples

- An employee steals customer data before resigning.
- A staff member accidentally emails confidential files to the wrong person.
- A former employee's account is never disabled.

## External Threats

An **external threat** comes from someone outside the organization.

They usually have no authorized access and must find a way to break in.

Examples include:

- Cybercriminals
- Hackers
- Hacktivists
- Nation-state attackers
- Malware operators

### Examples

- A ransomware attack.
- A phishing campaign.
- A hacker exploiting a vulnerable web server.
- A DDoS attack.

## Internal vs External

| Internal Threat | External Threat |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Comes from inside the organization | Comes from outside the organization |
| Already has authorized access | Must gain unauthorized access |
| Employees, contractors, former employees | Hackers, cybercriminals, hacktivists, nation-state attackers |

**Important:** Internal threats are often more dangerous because insiders already have legitimate access to company resources.


# 1.4.4 What Do You Think?

This scenario builds on the earlier phishing example.

During the investigation, the company discovered that:

- A former employee's account was **never removed**.
- The former employee had recently logged into the company's database.
- The employee now worked for a competitor.

## Exam Question

**Q: Is this an internal or external threat?**

### Correct Answer

**Internal Threat**

### Why?

Even though the person **no longer works for the company**, they were using an **internal account** that should have been disabled.

The organization failed to remove the employee's access after they left.

This is considered an **internal threat** because the attacker abused legitimate internal access.

### Lesson Learned

Organizations should always:

- Disable accounts immediately when employees leave.
- Review user permissions regularly.
- Follow the **Principle of Least Privilege**.
- Monitor login activity for unusual behavior.

Failing to remove old accounts creates an easy opportunity for attackers to gain access without needing to hack into the system.
