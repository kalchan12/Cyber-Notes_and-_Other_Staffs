# 4.2.1 Behavior-Based Security

## What is Behavior-Based Security?

Behavior-based security detects threats by **monitoring normal user and network activity**. Instead of looking for known malware signatures, it looks for **unusual or suspicious behavior (anomalies)** that may indicate an attack.

For example:

- An employee normally logs in between **8 AM and 5 PM**.
    
- Suddenly, their account logs in at **3 AM** from another country.
    
- This unusual behavior is flagged for investigation.
    

### Why is it Useful?

Traditional security looks for **known threats**.

Behavior-based security looks for **abnormal behavior**, making it effective against:

- Zero-day attacks
    
- Insider threats
    
- Unknown malware
    

### Remember

> **Normal behavior = Baseline**  
> **Unusual behavior = Anomaly**

An anomaly doesn't always mean an attack, but it should always be investigated.

# 4.2.2 NetFlow

## What is NetFlow?

NetFlow is a Cisco technology that **collects and analyzes network traffic information**.

It helps administrators understand:

- Who is using the network.
    
- Which devices are connected.
    
- Where traffic is coming from.
    
- Where traffic is going.
    
- How much traffic is being generated.
    

This information helps identify suspicious activity.

## How NetFlow Works

1. Routers, switches, and firewalls collect traffic information.
    
2. They send this information to a **NetFlow Collector**.
    
3. The collector stores and analyzes the data.
    
4. A normal traffic **baseline** is created.
    
5. Any unusual behavior is detected as an anomaly.
    

## Example

Normally:

- HR computer → Payroll server
    

Suddenly:

- HR computer → Hundreds of unknown IP addresses
    

This abnormal traffic may indicate malware or data theft.

### Remember

NetFlow **doesn't stop attacks**.

It helps administrators **monitor, analyze, and detect suspicious network behavior.**

# 4.2.3 Penetration Testing

## What is Penetration Testing?

Penetration Testing (Pen Testing) is an **authorized simulated cyber attack** performed to discover security weaknesses before real attackers do.

Instead of waiting for hackers to find vulnerabilities, ethical hackers actively search for them first.

The goal is **not to damage the system**, but to improve its security.

## Five Steps of a Pen Test

### 1. Planning

Define:

- Scope of the test
    
- Target systems
    
- Rules of engagement
    
- Testing objectives
    

### 2. Scanning

Gather information about the target.

Examples:

- Open ports
    
- Running services
    
- Operating systems
    
- Network structure
    

### 3. Gaining Access

Attempt to exploit discovered vulnerabilities to see whether attackers could break in.

### 4. Maintaining Access

Determine whether attackers could remain inside the system without being detected.

This simulates attacks like **Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs).**

### 5. Analysis & Reporting

Document:

- Vulnerabilities found
    
- Risk level
    
- How they were exploited
    
- Recommended fixes
    

### Remember

A penetration test is simply:

> **Hack your own system—with permission—to make it stronger.**

# 4.2.5 Impact Reduction

## What Happens After a Security Breach?

No organization can completely eliminate cyber attacks.

The goal is to **reduce the damage and recover quickly** when one occurs.

## Best Practices After a Breach

### Communicate the Issue

Inform affected customers, employees, and stakeholders as soon as possible.

### Be Honest and Accountable

Accept responsibility and explain what is being done to fix the problem.

### Provide Clear Details

Tell users:

- What happened
    
- What data was affected
    
- What actions they should take
    

### Find the Root Cause

Investigate how the attack happened so it doesn't happen again.

### Apply Lessons Learned

Improve security based on what the incident revealed.

### Verify Everything

After fixing the issue:

- Test systems
    
- Monitor for further attacks
    
- Confirm the vulnerability has been removed
    

### Educate Users

Teach employees how to recognize similar attacks in the future.

### Remember

A breach response isn't just about fixing computers—it's also about **protecting customer trust**.

# 4.2.6 What Is Risk Management?

## What is Risk Management?

Risk Management is the **continuous process of identifying, assessing, and reducing security risks**.

The goal isn't to eliminate every risk—that's impossible.

Instead, organizations try to reduce risk to an **acceptable level** while balancing security costs.

### Important Principle

> The cost of protecting an asset should **never be greater than the value of the asset itself.**

For example:

- Spending **$10,000** to protect **$500** worth of data doesn't make business sense.
    

## Four Steps of Risk Management

### 1. Frame the Risk

Identify:

- Valuable assets
    
- Possible threats
    
- Existing vulnerabilities
    

Understand what needs protection.

### 2. Assess the Risk

Determine:

- How likely the threat is.
    
- How serious the impact would be.
    

This helps prioritize which risks need immediate attention.

### 3. Respond to the Risk

Choose how to handle the risk.

Common responses include:

- Reduce it (add security controls)
    
- Avoid it
    
- Transfer it (e.g., insurance)
    
- Accept it (if the risk is low)
    

### 4. Monitor the Risk

Continuously review and update risk assessments because:

- New threats appear.
    
- Systems change.
    
- Businesses grow.
    

Risk management is an ongoing process—not a one-time task.

### Exam Tip

Remember the four steps in order:

1. **Frame** – Identify the risk.
    
2. **Assess** – Evaluate the likelihood and impact.
    
3. **Respond** – Decide how to handle it.
    
4. **Monitor** – Continuously review and improve.
    

A simple way to remember them is:

**Frame → Assess → Respond → Monitor (FARM)**.
